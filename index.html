Lecture 01

Introduction to Professional Skills

Core Concepts

  • Values, beliefs, attitudes, and character shape professional behavior.
  • Employability skills include communication, teamwork, problem solving, adaptability, time management, and leadership.
  • Professional skills are transferable abilities that support workplace success and career advancement.
  • The Johari Window supports awareness of strengths, blind spots, and feedback.
  • Technical, soft, and transferable skills work together to create a complete professional profile.
  • Continuous learning, practice, feedback, and accepting challenges are key ways to improve professional skills.
  • Professional identity is built through consistent actions, not only through qualifications or certificates.
  • Reflection helps turn experience into improvement by making strengths and weaknesses visible.

Practical Learning

I can use this lecture as a checklist for my own growth: identify the skills I already have, notice the skills that need practice, and ask for feedback from lecturers, classmates, and project team members.

Additional Notes

A professional profile is built through consistent behavior, so punctuality, ownership of tasks, and a willingness to improve from feedback matter just as much as technical ability.

Worksheet Practice

  • List three professional skills you already use well and three that still need practice.
  • Use the Johari Window idea to identify one blind spot and one strength you can share with your team.

My Reflection

This lecture helped me understand that technical knowledge alone is not enough for employability. I need to keep developing my attitude, values, communication, and teamwork because these qualities decide how effectively I work with others.

It also made me think about character as a long-term habit. Being responsible, respectful, and honest in small academic tasks can prepare me for the expectations of a professional workplace.

This means I should treat everyday classwork, deadlines, and team contributions as part of my professional image. The way I handle small responsibilities now will shape how others trust me later.

If I want to become more professional, I need to keep adjusting my habits over time instead of only focusing on the final result.

Lecture 02

Emotional Intelligence

Core Concepts

  • Emotional intelligence is the ability to perceive, understand, use, and manage emotions.
  • EI supports relationships, work performance, stress management, and leadership development.
  • Key EI domains include self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills.
  • IQ and EQ work together, but EQ is especially important when dealing with conflict, change, and pressure.
  • Emotions can be shown through voice, breathing, facial expression, muscle tone, energy level, and movement.
  • Self-regulation can be improved through mindfulness, cognitive re-appraisal, acceptance, and problem solving.
  • Active listening helps reduce emotional tension because it shows the speaker that their point is being understood.
  • Empathy helps people respond to each other with patience instead of assuming the worst in a stressful moment.

Practical Learning

In presentations and group assignments, I can pause before reacting, reframe negative thoughts, and listen for the emotion behind what another person is saying.

Additional Notes

In day-to-day teamwork, emotional intelligence shows up when I stay calm during disagreement, keep my tone respectful, and avoid taking feedback personally.

Worksheet Practice

  • Describe a situation where you felt stressed and write one way to respond more calmly next time.
  • Match one emotion with a practical response that would help a group discussion stay productive.

My Reflection

I learned that emotional control is a practical professional skill. In group work and presentations, I can perform better by recognizing my emotions, listening to others, responding calmly, and showing empathy when there is pressure or disagreement.

This lecture is especially relevant to software project work because deadlines and bugs can create stress. Emotional intelligence helps me stay constructive instead of blaming others or avoiding the problem.

It also reminded me that feedback is easier to accept when I do not react defensively. If I manage my emotions well, I can turn even criticism into useful guidance for improvement.

This is useful in teamwork because a calm response can stop a small problem from becoming a larger conflict and keeps the group focused on the task.

Source material: Emotional Intelligence (1).pptx.

Lecture 03

Portfolio Management

Core Concepts

  • Portfolios can be personal, career/dossier, employment, or assessment focused.
  • A strong portfolio has a clear purpose, defined audience, organized materials, and professional presentation.
  • Evidence can include a CV, projects, certificates, work samples, references, and reflective statements.
  • Digital tools such as LinkedIn, Google Sites, WordPress, Wix, Behance, and GitHub Pages can host portfolio work.
  • Portfolio content should be organized with clear sections such as introduction, CV, education, projects, skills, and reflections.
  • Each artifact should include context explaining why it is included and what was learned from it.
  • Regular updates keep the portfolio relevant and show steady professional growth over time.
  • Strong portfolios balance evidence, explanation, and presentation so the viewer can understand the story quickly.

Practical Learning

A portfolio should be updated continuously, not only at assignment deadlines. I can add new projects, screenshots, certificates, and reflections whenever I complete meaningful work.

Additional Notes

A strong portfolio should show context as well as output, so each item should explain the challenge, the tools used, and the result.

Worksheet Practice

  • Choose one project and write a short evidence note explaining why it belongs in your portfolio.
  • Plan one portfolio update you can add after your next assignment or project milestone.

My Reflection

This lecture changed how I see a portfolio. It is not only a place to display finished work; it is evidence of learning, progress, and self-assessment. I should update this portfolio regularly as my projects and professional skills improve.

For my career as a software engineering student, this website can become proof of both technical ability and personal development if I keep it organized, current, and focused on evidence.

I also learned that a portfolio is strongest when it tells a story. Instead of only listing achievements, I should connect each item to a clear skill, outcome, or learning experience.

That story becomes stronger when I include dates, responsibilities, and tools, because those details show the progression behind the finished work.

Source materials: Portfolio management.pptx, portfolio context document.

Lecture 04

CV and Cover Letter Writing

Core Concepts

  • A CV summarizes education, work experience, skills, achievements, and suitability for a role.
  • Effective CVs use clear formatting, headings, bullet points, white space, and consistent structure.
  • Key CV sections include personal information, professional summary, work experience, education, skills, and achievements.
  • Tailoring a CV requires researching the company, reading the job description, and highlighting relevant qualifications.
  • A professional summary should clearly show career goals, key skills, and value offered to the employer.
  • Common mistakes include spelling errors, irrelevant information, poor formatting consistency, and overly long descriptions.
  • Cover letters work best when they explain motivation, fit, and a few relevant achievements rather than repeating the CV word for word.
  • Proofreading matters because small errors can weaken a strong application even when the content is solid.

Practical Learning

Before applying for a role, I should compare my CV with the job description, highlight relevant projects, use action verbs, and make sure the document is easy to scan.

Additional Notes

A cover letter should not repeat the CV. It should connect one or two achievements directly to the job and explain why I want the role.

Worksheet Practice

  • Rewrite one CV bullet using a stronger action verb and a measurable result.
  • Draft one short cover letter paragraph that explains why you fit a specific role.

My Reflection

The CV lecture helped me improve how I present my software projects and technical skills. I learned to keep the CV clear, honest, and targeted so recruiters can quickly understand my strengths and suitability for a specific opportunity.

I also learned that a CV creates the first impression before an interview. Because of that, every section should support my purpose and avoid unnecessary details that distract from my strengths.

A strong CV is not just a summary of what I have done; it is a targeted document that proves why I am suitable for a specific opportunity.

I also learned that editing is part of writing. Removing weak or irrelevant details can improve the overall clarity of the document.

Source materials: CV writing.pptx, CV and Cover letter writing.docx.

Lecture 05

Job Hunting and Interview Skills

Core Concepts

  • Job interviews are conversations where both employer and candidate gather information and evidence.
  • Preparation includes knowing the job, researching the company, identifying strengths, and preparing questions.
  • Verbal and non-verbal communication influence the impression created during an interview.
  • Active listening, the 6 Cs of effective communication, and the STAR method help structure strong answers.
  • Successful candidates are specific, use company knowledge, maintain eye contact, ask questions, and support claims with examples.
  • Mock interview evaluation covers dress, posture, eye contact, calmness, clear speech, enthusiasm, and relevant questions.
  • Follow-up messages after the interview can reinforce interest and show professionalism.
  • Interview success also depends on showing readiness to learn in the new role.

Practical Learning

I can prepare interview answers by selecting real examples from my projects, then structuring them with Situation, Target, Action, and Result so my answers stay brief and complete.

Additional Notes

If possible, I should include results in my answers, such as improved speed, a solved problem, or a better outcome for the team, because concrete results are easier to trust.

Worksheet Practice

  • Prepare one STAR answer for a teamwork example and one for a problem-solving example.
  • Write two questions you would ask an interviewer about the team, tools, or growth opportunities.

My Reflection

I learned that interview confidence comes from preparation rather than memorizing answers. I can use the STAR structure to explain my project experience clearly and support my answers with specific examples from my software development work.

The lecture also showed me that the interview is two-way. I should prepare questions about the role, team, and organization so I can decide whether the opportunity matches my goals.

I learned that an interview is not about sounding perfect. It is about showing preparation, honesty, and the ability to communicate real experience in a structured way.

Rehearsing out loud can also help me notice filler words and refine how clearly I explain my experience under pressure.

Source materials: Job Hunting (1).pptx, Job Interviews-1.pptx, interview skills document.

Lecture 06

Email and Telephone Etiquette

Core Concepts

  • Telephone etiquette requires clear greetings, polite tone, careful hold/transfer handling, accurate messages, and professional closing.
  • Callers should not hear side conversations, office discussions, eating, chewing, or careless language.
  • Formal emails need a clear subject, appropriate greeting, organized body, polite closing, and full name.
  • Professional email communication avoids contractions, slang, full capital words, unnecessary exclamation marks, and informal signatures.
  • Messages should include complete details such as caller name, contact number, date, time, message, and required action.
  • Email writing practice includes thank-you messages, follow-ups, formal business updates, apologies, feedback, and condolences.
  • Timely replies matter because delayed responses can create confusion or make the sender feel ignored.
  • Clear subject lines help the receiver understand priority before opening the message.

Practical Learning

I should plan the purpose of an email or phone call before I start, use polite wording, and make the next action clear so the receiver does not need to guess what I mean.

Additional Notes

Small habits such as confirming names, repeating important details, and ending with a clear action point can prevent mistakes and save time for everyone involved.

Worksheet Practice

  • Write a formal email subject and opening line for a lecturer request.
  • Practice a short phone message that includes the caller name, number, time, and action needed.

My Reflection

This lecture made me more aware that small communication habits affect professional trust. I should write emails with a clear purpose and speak on calls with patience, accuracy, and respect, especially when representing a team or organization.

As a student, this is useful when contacting lecturers, teammates, clients, or internship recruiters. Professional communication can show reliability even before any technical work is reviewed.

I also learned that communication etiquette is about respect and accuracy at the same time. A polite message still needs clear details so the receiver can act without needing a second follow-up.

That matters because communication is often judged by how easy it is for the other person to respond, not only by how polite it sounds.

Source materials: Telephone-Etiquette.pdf, email-etiquette.pdf, email_use.pdf, Email Writing Worksheet.docx.

Lecture 07

Dining Etiquette and Table Manners

Core Concepts

  • Dining etiquette is about making others comfortable, not acting perfect.
  • Core habits include phone-free tables, napkin use, chewing before speaking, pacing with others, and respectful conversation.
  • Formal dining includes table-setting awareness, utensil order, host cues, posture, seating protocol, and multi-course pacing.
  • Cultural dining differences require respect, observation, and adaptability.
  • Dining conversation should focus on appropriate topics such as shared interests, travel, books, projects, and local events.
  • Modern etiquette includes digital payment awareness, QR menu use, food waste awareness, and handling online reviews respectfully.
  • Good table manners also help build trust during networking meals and formal events.
  • Observing the host and the pace of others is important because it helps you follow the flow of the meal without creating attention.

Practical Learning

I can apply this by being present during meals, keeping my phone away, waiting for the host or others before starting, and choosing polite conversation topics.

Additional Notes

Dining events can also be networking opportunities, so calm timing, neat table habits, and awareness of the group help create a better impression.

Worksheet Practice

  • List three table manners that help keep a meal comfortable for everyone.
  • Write one example of a safe conversation topic and one topic to avoid at a formal meal.

My Reflection

I learned that dining etiquette is useful in business meals, networking events, and professional social situations. Being present, polite, and aware of others can help me make a better impression outside technical environments too.

The key lesson for me is that etiquette reduces discomfort. If I understand basic table manners, I can focus on building relationships instead of worrying about what to do next.

This matters because a meal can become part of a professional conversation. When I behave appropriately, I can focus on the interaction itself rather than on avoiding mistakes.

I also learned that table manners are a form of social awareness. They show that I can respect shared space, read timing, and keep the setting comfortable for everyone involved.

Source material: dining-etiquettes.pptx.

Lecture 08

Grooming and Etiquette

Core Concepts

  • Grooming means maintaining a clean, neat, appropriate, and well-presented appearance.
  • Etiquette means socially appropriate behavior, including greetings, listening, phone use, group behavior, and respect for personal space.
  • First impressions are formed through appearance, posture, tone of voice, punctuality, greeting style, and confidence.
  • Professional behavior includes respecting diversity, personal boundaries, digital boundaries, and inclusive communication.
  • Personal branding is the repeated impression created through habits, communication, behavior, digital presence, and consistency.
  • Daily grooming includes body hygiene, hand hygiene, oral care, hair care, neat clothing, clean shoes, and moderate fragrance.
  • Consistency between online presence and in-person conduct strengthens a professional image.
  • Small details such as posture, facial expression, and the way you carry documents also shape how professionally you are perceived.

Practical Learning

Before interviews, presentations, or networking events, I should prepare my outfit, check grooming details, arrange documents, and arrive early enough to enter calmly.

Additional Notes

Grooming also includes digital presence. Profile photos, usernames, and short bios can influence first impressions before I even speak.

Worksheet Practice

  • Check your digital profile and note one improvement that would make it more professional.
  • Write a short grooming checklist for an interview or presentation day.

My Reflection

This lecture reminded me that professionalism starts before I speak. Clean presentation, punctuality, respectful greetings, and careful listening can support my personal brand and help others feel comfortable working with me.

I also learned that etiquette is connected to respect. Respecting names, accents, cultures, personal space, and digital boundaries is part of being ready for a diverse workplace.

This lecture helped me see that grooming is not only about looking presentable. It also affects how confidently and respectfully I enter a space and how professionally I am remembered afterward.

Another useful idea is that grooming supports discipline. When I prepare myself properly, I am also preparing my mindset to act carefully and responsibly in that environment.

Lecture 09

Negotiation Skills

Core Concepts

  • Negotiation is a structured process for reaching a mutually acceptable agreement.
  • Key principles include people before problems, trust and verify, focus on interests, invest in options, and co-own the process.
  • Preparation includes building relationships, setting goals, knowing BATNA, strategizing, and being ready to improvise.
  • Effective negotiation uses active listening, questioning, rapport, strategic empathy, and win-win thinking.
  • BATNA is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement and acts as a strategic anchor during negotiation.
  • Common mistakes include lack of preparation, ignoring BATNA, emotional reactions, cognitive biases, and poor communication.
  • Objective criteria and compromise help turn disagreement into practical agreement.
  • Good negotiators also know when to pause, reframe the issue, and return with clearer information instead of forcing a rushed decision.

Practical Learning

Before any negotiation, I should list my alternatives, decide my walk-away point, understand the other party's interests, and prepare options that can create value for both sides.

Additional Notes

A good negotiation keeps the focus on the problem, not the person, and uses objective criteria so both sides can justify the final outcome.

Worksheet Practice

  • List your BATNA for one project or academic negotiation.
  • Write one win-win option that could satisfy both sides of a disagreement.

My Reflection

I learned that negotiation is not just arguing for my side. It is a prepared conversation where I should understand interests, know my alternatives, listen actively, and look for outcomes that protect both objectives and relationships.

This can help me in project teams when dividing tasks, managing deadlines, or discussing design decisions. A good negotiation should solve the problem without damaging the working relationship.

The lecture also showed me that negotiation is useful even in small academic settings, because it teaches me how to explain my point clearly while still listening to other views.

I think this is important because negotiation is not only about getting a result. It is also about controlling tone, timing, and trust so the agreement can last after the conversation ends.

Lecture 10

Business Ethics in Practice

Core Concepts

  • Professionals have a duty to uphold high standards of conduct, integrity, and public interest.
  • Fundamental principles include integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, and professional behavior.
  • Ethical threats include self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, and intimidation threats.
  • Professional judgment uses facts, consultation, bias awareness, the reasonable informed third party test, and safeguards.
  • Safeguards include internal controls, reporting channels, documentation, escalation, and whistleblowing when needed.
  • Ethical dilemmas often involve pressure, ambiguity, conflicting responsibilities, and no simple right-or-wrong answer.
  • Transparency and accountability help preserve trust when professionals make difficult decisions.
  • Ethical practice also requires reviewing consequences, because a decision that feels convenient in the moment can still create long-term harm.

Practical Learning

In software work, I should protect user data, document important decisions, avoid misleading claims, and ask for guidance when a situation creates a conflict of interest or pressure to act wrongly.

Additional Notes

Ethics is practical in everyday software work because privacy, honesty, and accountability are part of normal decisions, not just special crisis situations.

Worksheet Practice

  • Describe one ethical threat that could affect a software project and one safeguard to reduce it.
  • Write one short example of how confidentiality should be protected in a student project.

My Reflection

This lecture showed me that ethical decisions affect trust and reputation. In future work, especially when handling data or decisions that affect others, I should avoid conflicts of interest, protect confidential information, and use proper reporting channels when something is wrong.

I also understood that ethics is not only about avoiding punishment. It is about protecting people, organizations, and the profession by making decisions that can be justified to a reasonable informed third party.

This lecture made it clear that ethical behavior is part of professional competence. If I act honestly and document decisions properly, I protect both users and my own credibility.

I also learned that ethics should not be treated as a separate topic from technical work. The way I build, communicate, and report work is part of the quality of the work itself.
